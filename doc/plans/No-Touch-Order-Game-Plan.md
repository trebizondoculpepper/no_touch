# No Touch Order

## Project brief

**Working title:** No Touch Order  
**Format:** Single-player, first-person, three-dimensional narrative game  
**Setting:** One small, deliberately ordinary police guardroom or checkpoint interior  
**Tone:** Steady bureaucratic deadpan, developing into a Kafkaesque nightmare  
**Primary reference:** The Stanley Parable, particularly its use of narration, player behaviour and disputed agency

The player has been appointed to guard a large red button. A sign says that it must not be pressed. Nobody else ever enters the room, so the only person capable of pressing it is the person employed to prevent that from happening.

An unidentified narrator explains that the player was selected for their exemplary moral character. Over successive shifts, administrative busywork, boredom, dreams and the narrator's insinuations turn a trivial prohibition into an obsession. Eventually the player presses the button. Nothing happens. The narrator explains that the button never did anything.

That apparent release is the beginning of the moral collapse. The button later appears damaged. An investigation gives the player opportunities to admit responsibility or conceal it. Staying at the post allows the institution to invent an outside enemy, invoke a loss of faith, and justify increasingly elaborate security measures. Finally, the player is rewarded for their supposed trustworthiness with a second button: one that controls an armed perimeter.

The person who could not be trusted with a harmless button is promoted to responsibility for a dangerous one.

## Creative intention

The game is a darkly comic political allegory about moral failure becoming institutional authority. One small surrender leads to concealment, scapegoating, moral righteousness, militarisation and promotion. The administrative system absorbs the protagonist's failures and converts them into evidence of loyalty and fitness for greater responsibility.

The political reference is the tendency of modern institutions, particularly in American politics, to reward visibly untrustworthy people with more power and to reinterpret abhorrent conduct as heroic service. This should emerge through the player's actions and the institution's responses rather than an explanatory political speech.

The protagonist really does press the button. The system supplies excuses and rewards afterward. Preserve that relationship: the institution does not need to fabricate the original transgression to exploit it.

The intended experience is a nightmare in which bad actions compound and inaction permits the situation to worsen. It is not necessary to give every branch an equally satisfying escape or to turn the story into a moral lesson with a correct answer.

## The room

The entire playable world occupies approximately ten metres by ten metres, with a normal ceiling height and a single exit. It resembles the interior of a modest police station, checkpoint or guard post. Its ordinariness is essential: the absurdity acquires weight because everyone treats the setting as a normal workplace.

### Established features

- A large red push-button on a column or pedestal, with the physical clarity and appeal of a Portal-style button.
- A prominently displayed instruction: **DO NOT PRESS THE BUTTON**.
- A wall clock, establishing the working day and the significance of 5 p.m.
- A hook for the protagonist's police coat and hat.
- Police signage in several languages, including English, German, French and Welsh.
- A desk or work surface for forms and routine administration.
- Equipment for scanning fingerprints and consulting a fingerprint database.
- A disposal chute for evidence that is recorded as having no match.
- Space for the later appearance of international bunting, religious elements and perimeter defences.

The police identity should be explicit, but the country and force should remain unspecified. Do not identify the organisation as ICE, the FBI or a particular national police service. Multilingual signage establishes a generic policing institution.

The opening shows the protagonist hanging up their own police coat and removing and hanging their hat. This establishes their role through an ordinary arrival ritual before the narrator explains the assignment.

### Suggested environmental treatment

Scuffed flooring, practical furniture, filing equipment, fluorescent lighting and institutional paint would suit the setting. These are art-direction suggestions rather than fixed requirements. Keep the room legible and avoid unnecessary props that imply a larger investigation or an unseen cast of visitors.

## Narration and presentation

The narrator's identity remains unresolved. It could be an external authority, a voice in the protagonist's head, or something between the two. Do not settle that question through an early explanation.

The voice remains calm, procedural and reassuring as events become increasingly disturbing. It can praise, tempt, minimise, accuse and absolve without acknowledging any contradiction. At times it supplies the protagonist's apparent thoughts or desires, especially during the pursuit dream.

The narrator is not merely describing events. It assigns meanings to actions, announces the institution's interpretation of evidence, and converts continued participation into apparent agreement.

Dialogue examples below preserve the intended meaning and flavour. They are draft lines, not a finished recording script. The creator intends to be able to perform the narration personally.

## Detailed story progression

### 1. Arrival and appointment

The protagonist arrives for work and hangs up their police coat and hat. The player takes control inside the guardroom. The red button and prohibition sign are immediately understandable.

The narrator explains the job: ensure that nobody presses the button. The player has been chosen because of their exemplary values, reliability and moral standing. Management already knows that this is someone who would never press it.

The assignment contains an obvious absurdity. Nobody is coming into the room. There is only the player. The solemnity of the appointment should remain completely disproportionate to the practical task.

### 2. Administrative routine

Guarding the button requires paperwork. The player completes an arrival form, provisionally called **Form A23**, and carries out routine checks and records. The work is redundant, dull and treated as indispensable.

The game does not run through a literal working day in real time. Tasks and narrative beats advance the clock. A short sequence of procedural actions can represent hours of uneventful duty.

The purpose of the busywork is threefold: establish the institution's normality, give the player physical actions within the room, and create the fatigue that leads into the dreams. It should communicate boredom without requiring hours of actual boredom.

### 3. Temptation presented as reassurance

The narrator begins discussing the button's tactile appeal while insisting that this could never interest the player.

> You've never touched a button before, have you? You wouldn't know what it's like: the click under your finger, the slight movement as the contacts connect, the surge of power as it reaches the bottom of its travel. That's not something you'd be interested in. That's why we chose you.

The praise makes the prohibition personal. Pressing the button would contradict the identity the narrator has assigned to the protagonist. At the same time, the description makes pressing it more imaginable and desirable.

### 4. The first lapse

During the routine, the protagonist apparently nods off. A blink or brief loss of continuity is followed by a camera movement suggesting waking, disorientation and a shake of the head.

The player discovers that they are unexpectedly close to the button, perhaps leaning over it. The precise pose remains to be designed; the essential beat is unexplained proximity after a lapse in awareness.

The narrator responds with indulgent concern. Everyone nods off occasionally. There is no need to worry. Just stay away from the button.

### 5. The affectionate button dream

The button begins to behave impossibly. Wherever the player looks, it moves into view, as though determined to be noticed. Little heart symbols appear around the pedestal. The button speaks:

> Go on. I don't mind if you press me. I like to be pressed. Pressing's fun.

This is eventually revealed to be a dream. The protagonist wakes. The button is an ordinary button again.

The narrator normalises the experience:

> You haven't been having the button dream, have you? Don't worry. Everyone who works here long enough gets the button dream. The important thing is that you don't act upon it.

This reassurance introduces an implied history of other employees without introducing anyone into the room.

### 6. Five o'clock and the off-duty loophole

The clock reaches 5 p.m. The narrator congratulates the player on another day during which no buttons have been pressed. The shift is over and the player may leave.

Then the narrator observes that the button is no longer the player's responsibility. It would be funny if somebody pressed it immediately after the guard went off duty.

The player can leave through the exit or remain and press the button. Leaving ends the shift and moves to another working day in the same room. It does not require a playable journey home or another location.

Successive refusals can produce variations on the end-of-day narration. The intended main story continues when the player eventually chooses to press. The duration and variation of this refusal loop remain design decisions.

### 7. The first press

The player presses the button. Nothing happens beyond the physical action itself.

The narrator explains that the button does nothing. It never did anything. The player can press it repeatedly; there will still be no external effect.

> Press it as many times as you want. The button never did anything. The button never mattered.

Allow this apparent deflation to land. The scene seems to expose the prohibition as meaningless, but it is not the ending. The player finishes the day and leaves.

### 8. The pursuit dream

On a subsequent shift, the player approaches the button, ostensibly to perform the daily inspection and establish whether it has been pressed.

The button physically moves away. It evades the player, maintaining distance and turning the approach into a chase inside the room.

The narrator supplies an increasingly possessive, flirtatious interpretation of the protagonist's pursuit:

> Oh, you sly little button. You little coquettish minx. You want to play with me? Shall I chase you? Is that what we're playing? Press the button?

This sequence is also revealed to be a dream. The button is just a button and could not possibly run away. Keep the literal movement within the dream rather than making a mobile button an established feature of the waking world.

### 9. Something is wrong with the button

The protagonist wakes to find that the real button looks different. It is slightly damaged and soiled. Some kind of unidentified fluid may be leaking from it.

The narrator notices its condition as though encountering the problem for the first time:

> The button seems a little off today, doesn't it? It couldn't have been you interfering with our button. But something definitely isn't right. Someone has been pressing this button. We should find out who.

The condition creates an uncomfortable connection to the preceding dream without resolving exactly what occurred. Do not supply an explicit account of the damage or identify the fluid at this stage.

### 10. Management requires an investigation

Management is unhappy. Someone has been pressing the button, and this is now a serious matter. The player is assigned to investigate the very transgression they have committed.

The investigation turns into another set of clerical tasks:

1. Take or scan fingerprints from the button.
2. Submit the prints to a database.
3. Review the result.
4. Complete a report identifying the suspect or recording that no usable identification was obtained.
5. If there is no match, dispose of the fingerprint evidence through the chute.

The proposed consequential choice is whether to acknowledge the prints as the protagonist's own or declare that none could be found. The relationship between collecting prints, a database match and reporting no evidence needs a precise interaction design. Preserve an intelligible opportunity to tell the truth or conceal it.

Destroying evidence should be a physical bureaucratic action, not merely a line of dialogue. The player handles the material and puts it into the disposal chute as part of an apparently normal procedure.

The exact outcome of an honest fingerprint report is not yet established. Do not assume that every report automatically produces the same institutional response.

### 11. Admission or continued duty

The narrator offers an exit framed as an admission of guilt:

> If it was you, walk out that door and never come back. You've disgraced yourself, but I'll let you leave with whatever dignity remains. If, as I strongly suspect, it wasn't you, approach the button. Stand resolute and make sure nobody comes near our precious button again.

Leaving means accepting responsibility in the terms the narrator has supplied. Staying means maintaining the claim to be a trustworthy guard. The precise presentation of the departure ending remains open.

This is distinct from leaving at the end of an ordinary shift. The story must make the difference clear.

### 12. The invention of foreigners

If the player remains at their post, the narrator announces that it knows who is responsible:

> It was the foreigners.

No foreigners have entered. Nobody else has entered at all. The narrator directs the player's attention to international flag bunting, presented as evidence that outsiders have been here.

The bunting is newly introduced. The narrator treats it as something that has been there all along. It is a pretext created after the accusation, not a previously planted clue that the player failed to solve.

The player is told to stay alert against foreigners who might come and press the institution's perfect buttons. Remaining at the post has now become participation in a racist explanation of the protagonist's own misconduct.

Do not require a separate dialogue option explicitly endorsing the accusation before this interpretation is imposed. The nightmare operates through continued participation and the narrator's appropriation of it.

### 13. The recovery of faith

The invented threat becomes the basis for a wider account of institutional decline. The narrator explains that foreigners have been getting away with pressing the buttons because the institution has lost its spirituality or sense of faith.

The solution is a recovery of faith. The institution that initially praised the protagonist's inherent moral standing now demands moral renewal, while leaving the underlying dishonesty intact.

This is an established story beat, but its physical tasks, visual symbols and any particular religious vocabulary have not been settled. It should develop naturally from the scapegoating rather than appearing as an unrelated religious joke.

### 14. Quadrupling the defences

The narrator declares that the button requires stronger protection: double it, or rather quadruple it.

A previously hidden lever appears or becomes accessible. Pulling it causes concentric defensive structures to descend or deploy around the original button. The ordinary pedestal becomes the centre of a miniature fortress inside the same room.

The proposed installation includes a square enclosure of wire mesh, barbed wire and an automatic cannon. The cannon tracks anyone who comes near, including the protagonist. Its movement makes the threat visible without requiring combat or the arrival of actual intruders.

The disproportion is central: an inert button is now protected by machinery that can cause real harm. This deployment is the result of an action performed by the player.

### 15. Promotion: the second button

With the original button secured, the narrator presents a second button that activates the perimeter defences.

The protagonist is again praised as exceptionally loyal, trustworthy and unlikely to press buttons inappropriately. On that basis, they are entrusted with this new solemn duty.

The language should recall the initial appointment closely enough for the reversal to register. The institution has processed a failure of trust into a qualification for greater power.

End the game on the entrustment of the second button. Whether the player can physically press it before the ending is an unresolved implementation choice. No further act or revelation is currently required.

## Player actions and narrative structure

The core action vocabulary is intentionally small:

- Walk, look and approach or retreat from objects.
- Inspect and interact with the button.
- Complete forms and routine checks.
- Scan fingerprints and consult the database.
- Report, file or dispose of evidence.
- Leave through the door when permitted.
- Pull the defence lever.

The narrative is a sequence of changing states of the same room. The principal progression is:

**Appointment → routine and temptation → dreams → off-duty choice → first press → pursuit dream → damage → investigation → denial and scapegoating → faith → fortification → promotion.**

Two different forms of departure must be represented: the routine end-of-shift exit that advances to another day, and the later departure that is framed as an admission of guilt. The game also needs to accommodate a player who presses early, repeatedly refuses, or interrupts narration. Their exact treatment is not yet specified.

## Production scope and guardrails

- Keep the world within the one room. Shifts, departures and dreams can use transitions and altered room states.
- Keep other people physically absent. Narration can refer to management and previous guards without showing them.
- Use changes to familiar objects to carry the escalation.
- Preserve the distinction between the inert original button and the dangerous defensive apparatus.
- Keep the narrator's bureaucratic register steady through the political and religious escalation.
- Let the player perform consequential procedures rather than reducing the experience to an uninterrupted monologue.
- Avoid explaining away the dreams, narrator or institutional contradictions through a final lore reveal.

No engine, platform, rendering style, runtime target or final duration has been selected. This is a narrative and interaction brief, not a technology commitment.

## Suggested first playable milestone

Build a rough version of the complete narrative route before investing heavily in polished assets. Use a simple room, placeholder props, temporary narration or subtitles, and basic interactions. Test whether completing ordinary procedures makes the escalation feel like a continuation of the same job.

The first playable should establish the appointment, one representative administrative task, a dream transition, the 5 p.m. choice, the inert first press, the investigation, the invented accusation, the faith beat, the deployment of defences and the second-button promotion. Refusal variations and secondary responses can follow once the central sequence works.

The defining final image is a guard who failed to protect one harmless button, standing beside the dangerous second button they have been rewarded with.
